0x00 Preface#

I got into optimization because I wanted to understand a few lines of convergence proof in a paper. I’d hit an inequality, look it up, then come back and see what it was doing there. After a few rounds of that I started keeping notes on the ones I kept seeing. If you’re stuck on any of the same steps, pick a section and work through it with me. Let me know if you get something different.

Here are a few symbols we’ll keep seeing. I’ll shorten the Euclidean norm $\Vert\cdot\Vert_2$ to $\Vert\cdot\Vert$, and usually look at $\min_{w\in\mathbb R^d}f(w)$. If the minimum is attained, pick a minimizer $w^{\ast}$ and write $f^{\ast}=f(w^{\ast})$ for its value. Sometimes all we have is a finite lower bound $f_{\inf}$, which will be enough for some of the proofs.

Deep network objectives are usually non-convex, so why spend time on convex functions? I want to start with a few cases we can work out and see how much each extra condition buys us in the error bound. When we go back to a training problem, we can check those conditions one at a time.

0x01 Fundamental Concepts#

Convex Function#

Start with the line segment between two points. For $f$ to be convex on the convex set $\mathcal D$, we take any $x,y\in\mathcal D$ and $\theta\in[0,1]$ and require the function value at the interpolated point to lie below the interpolation of the two endpoint values.

$$ f(\theta x+(1-\theta)y)\le\theta f(x)+(1-\theta)f(y). $$

In the proofs I keep coming back to this first-order form. When $f$ is differentiable, picture its tangent plane at one point. At another point the function value has to stay above that plane.

$$ f(y)\ge f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle. $$

At a point where the function is non-differentiable, look for a subgradient $g\in\partial f(x)$. It gives us the same kind of lower bound, with $f(y)\ge f(x)+\langle g,y-x\rangle$ for every $y$.

$\mu$-strongly Convex#

defination

Strong convexity adds a quadratic term to the first-order lower bound. For a differentiable function and a given $\mu$ with $\mu>0$, we write the definition as follows.
$$ f(y)\ge f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+\frac{\mu}{2}\|y-x\|^2. $$
At a non-smooth point, use a subgradient $g\in\partial f(x)$ in place of the gradient $\nabla f(x)$.

There’s another way I find easier to remember. To check whether $f$ is $\mu$-strongly convex, take the quadratic term back out and see if $f(x)-\mu\Vert x\Vert^2/2$ is convex. This version uses the squared Euclidean norm.

We’ll use both inequalities below. If $w^{\ast}$ is an unconstrained minimizer of a differentiable function, then $\nabla f(w^{\ast})=0$. Apply strong convexity with the minimizer first as the point of expansion and then as the comparison point.

$$ f(w)-f^*\ge\frac{\mu}{2}\|w-w^*\|^2, \qquad \langle\nabla f(w),w-w^*\rangle \ge f(w)-f^*+\frac{\mu}{2}\|w-w^*\|^2. $$

Lipschitz Continuous#

We’ll run into two Lipschitz conditions. I write “function values” next to this first one. The $G$-Lipschitz condition tells us how far apart the values can be when the inputs are a given distance apart.

$$ |f(x)-f(y)|\le G\|x-y\|. $$

For a differentiable function on the whole space, looking along a line segment relates this condition to the uniform gradient bound $\Vert\nabla f(x)\Vert\le G$. For non-smooth convex functions the corresponding bound is on subgradients. On a constrained domain, we need to say which subgradients we use at the boundary.

Smoothness#

defination

Now look at the gradient itself. A differentiable function $f$ is $L$-smooth when its gradient is $L$-Lipschitz.
$$ \|\nabla f(x)-\nabla f(y)\|\le L\|x-y\|. $$

This is how smoothness usually makes its way into a convergence proof. Use the gradient where you start to estimate the function value at the next point. The descent lemma bounds how far off that estimate can be.

$$ \left|f(y)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle\right| \le\frac{L}{2}\|y-x\|^2. $$

If you want to see where the quadratic comes from, follow me along the segment. Put the difference between the gradients inside the integral and bound it by smoothness. That’s where the constant comes from.

$$ \begin{aligned} f(y)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle &=\int_0^1\langle\nabla f(x+s(y-x))-\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle\,ds,\\ \left|f(y)-f(x)-\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle\right| &\le\int_0^1 Ls\|y-x\|^2\,ds =\frac L2\|y-x\|^2. \end{aligned} $$

For a differentiable convex function, the one-sided quadratic upper bound also gives Lipschitz continuity of the gradient. To see what changes for non-convex functions, I find it helpful to try $f(x)=-x^4$. Its tangents already sit above the graph, so the one-sided upper bound holds for any positive $L$. Its gradient changes faster and faster as we move away from the origin. I’ll use the gradient definition of smoothness in the non-convex sections.

0x02 Optimization Methods#

Gradient Descent#

$$ w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta_t\nabla f(w_t),\qquad\eta_t>0. $$

The step size can stay fixed or vary. Try expanding the squared distance once here. When the gradient times a parameter difference turns up later, you can come back and see how to rewrite it using the two squared distances and a squared gradient term.

$$ \|w_{t+1}-w^*\|^2 =\|w_t-w^*\|^2-2\eta_t\langle\nabla f(w_t),w_t-w^*\rangle +\eta_t^2\|\nabla f(w_t)\|^2. $$

Stochastic and Mini-batch Gradient Descent#

For SGD I keep two losses apart. The sample we just drew has loss $\ell(w;\xi)$, and the objective we want to minimize is $f(w)=\mathbb E_\xi[\ell(w;\xi)]$. If we can interchange differentiation and expectation, draw a batch of independent samples, compute their gradients, and take a step using the average.

$$ g_t=\frac1b\sum_{r=1}^b\nabla\ell(w_t;\xi_{t,r}),\qquad w_{t+1}=w_t-\eta_tg_t. $$

With a single sample, $b=1$, we recover single-sample SGD. The case $b>1$ gives mini-batch SGD. Using $\ell$ for one sample’s loss makes it easier to keep track of what we’re averaging when we take expectations.

0x03 Common Assumptions#

I’ve put the assumptions together here. If you get stuck on a bound later, come back and see which of these the argument used.

Bounded Domain and Bounded Subgradients#

First, look at where the constraint is imposed. On a closed convex feasible set $\mathcal C$, we can bound its diameter by $D$, which means that any two points in the set satisfy $\Vert x-y\Vert\le D$. That number bounds the region where the iterates can move.

In non-smooth analysis we often also assume $\Vert g_t\Vert\le G$ for the subgradient chosen at each step. There’s a trap when writing the assumptions together. Strong convexity forces at least quadratic growth over the whole space, while a Lipschitz function grows at most linearly. To use both conditions, put the subgradient bound on the feasible set or on the trajectory and explain why the iterates stay there.

Conditional Unbiasedness and Bounded Variance#

Picture arriving at the current step before drawing any new data. Everything that’s already happened goes into $\mathcal F_t$. Then $w_t$ is measurable with respect to $\mathcal F_t$, so the current parameter is fixed for this conditional expectation. Assume the new samples are independent given that history and satisfy the two conditions below.

$$ \mathbb E[\nabla\ell(w_t;\xi_{t,r})\mid\mathcal F_t]=\nabla f(w_t), \qquad \mathbb E[\|\nabla\ell(w_t;\xi_{t,r})-\nabla f(w_t)\|^2\mid\mathcal F_t]\le\sigma^2. $$

Average the gradients in the batch, then work out the mean and the squared norm separately.

$$ \mathbb E[g_t\mid\mathcal F_t]=\nabla f(w_t),\qquad \mathbb E[\|g_t-\nabla f(w_t)\|^2\mid\mathcal F_t]\le\frac{\sigma^2}{b}, $$ $$ \mathbb E[\|g_t\|^2\mid\mathcal F_t] \le\|\nabla f(w_t)\|^2+\frac{\sigma^2}{b}. $$

To see the factor $1/b$, try expanding the squared norm of the average of just two noise vectors. The cross terms have zero conditional mean, leaving the squared terms for the two vectors. A whole batch works the same way. The variance here is the expected squared norm. With repeated or correlated samples, go back and account for the cross terms.

0x04 Common Inequalities#

Cauchy-Schwarz and Triangle Inequalities#

$$ |\langle x,y\rangle|\le\|x\|\|y\|,\qquad \|x+y\|\le\|x\|+\|y\|. $$

Jensen Inequality#

Jensen’s inequality comes up whenever we average gradients or parameters. Start with convex $f$ and weights satisfying $\theta_i\ge0$ and $\sum_i\theta_i=1$.

$$ f\left(\sum_i\theta_ix_i\right)\le\sum_i\theta_if(x_i). $$

With equal weights I usually write $f(\bar x)\le n^{-1}\sum_i f(x_i)$, where $\bar x=n^{-1}\sum_i x_i$. Keep the weights with the average as you manipulate it. As a quick check, take $f(x)=x^2$ and two identical nonzero points. The inequality $f(n\bar x)\le nf(\bar x)$ goes the other way.

Polyak–Łojasiewicz (PL) Inequality#

defination

To get a linear rate later, we'll need to relate the squared gradient norm to the function-value gap. That's where the PL condition comes in.
$$ \|\nabla f(x)\|^2\ge2\mu(f(x)-f^*). $$
A differentiable, unconstrained $\mu$-strongly convex function satisfies PL. In the later proofs, keep an eye on this one relation. Even for a non-convex objective, we can use it in the corresponding recursion if we establish the inequality separately.

If you’re working from strong convexity to PL and get stuck here, try fixing $x$. The lower bound for arbitrary $y$ then gives us an expression we know how to minimize.

$$ f(y)\ge f(x)+\langle\nabla f(x),y-x\rangle+\frac\mu2\|y-x\|^2. $$

All that’s left is to minimize the quadratic on the right in $y$, whose minimum is $f(x)-\Vert\nabla f(x)\Vert^2/(2\mu)$. Taking the infimum on both sides gives $f^{\ast}\ge f(x)-\Vert\nabla f(x)\Vert^2/(2\mu)$. Move the squared gradient norm across and we have the PL inequality we wanted.
