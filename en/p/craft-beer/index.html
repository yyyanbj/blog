Preface#

Craft beer really is my favorite kind of drink. Without the high ABV of cocktails or the monotony of light industrial lagers, it offers an impressively rich range of flavors and textures.

What draws me in most is that sheer variety of flavor. Light “water beer” is usually just something to wash down food, while cocktails tend to be high in alcohol. Sip an iced drink too slowly and the melting ice waters it down; drink it too fast and it’s easy to lose track of how much you’ve had. Craft beer gives you plenty of room to choose—whether you’re after crisp malt, roasted coffee and cocoa notes, hop bitterness, or tart, sweet, and fruity aromas, there’s a good chance you’ll find something to your taste. Of course, that also depends on what the bar happens to have on tap.

I usually like to take 30–50 minutes over a glass of craft beer. Drink it too fast and you miss the subtle details; leave it sitting too long and it loses its original character.

My personal favorite style is stout, followed by IPA, then lagers and wheat beers, with fruit purées further down the list. Sour beer really isn’t my thing—to me it always tastes a bit like red wine.

Beyond what’s in the glass, I love the vibe of a craft beer bar. Sitting down with friends, talking, and stretching one glass over half the evening feels effortless. Sitting at the bar counter is a different kind of fun too—every now and then a casual chat turns into meeting someone interesting.

Beer talk is always welcome. 🍺

Below are some craft beer notes I had GPT help pull together—happy to share them with you.

What Exactly Is Craft Beer?#

Before talking about “craft beer,” it helps to look at how the phrase is actually used. Different regions draw the line differently. The definition people cite most often comes from the Brewers Association in the US—though it was really written for industry statistics to define American craft brewers by size and independence, not a specific style in the glass. 1

In everyday conversation, though, most of us care a lot more about what we’re tasting. Swap the malt, hops, or yeast—or add fruit, spices, or barrel aging—and the flavor shifts dramatically. A few things I keep in mind when scanning a menu:

  • IPAs tend to dominate tap lists, but look a little further down and the same brewery often pours lagers, wheat beers, and other traditional styles.
  • Craft beer doesn’t have to be bitter or heavy; if you want something crisp and easy, there are plenty of low-IBU, lower-ABV options.
  • Haziness, filtration, and adjuncts are just choices of process and style, not a verdict on quality—a crystal-clear beer can have plenty of depth, and well-used adjuncts can be great too.
  • Fancy labels and price tags are fun to glance at, but whether a beer works for you still comes down to what’s in the glass.

With a beer I haven’t tried before, I usually just ask about the style and how fresh it is, take a sip to see how the flavors hang together, and go from there.

Where Does a Beer’s Flavor Come From?#

Four Basic Ingredients#

If you ever wonder where all those unexpected flavors come from, start with the core four: water, malt, hops, and yeast. Plenty of breweries also throw in unmalted grains, sugar, fruit, or spices, depending on the recipe.

IngredientPrimary RoleFlavor or Mouthfeel to Watch For
WaterForms the brewing base; its mineral profile shapes both mashing and flavorThe same recipe brewed with different water can feel softer or sharper in bitterness
MaltSupplies starch for mashing and sets the color and core flavorGrain, bread, biscuit, caramel; darker malts bring coffee or roasted notes
HopsAdds bitterness along with layers of aroma and flavorFloral, herbal, citrus, pine, or tropical fruit notes
YeastTurns fermentable sugars into alcohol and CO₂ while creating fermentation flavorsSome strains bring bold fruit or spice; others finish clean and neutral

When a glass smells strongly of fruit or coffee, don’t assume juice or coffee beans went into the tank—a quick check of the ingredient list usually clears it up, since fermentation and dark-roasted malt produce a lot of those aromas on their own. 2

A Simplified Look at Brewing#

Boiled down to one sentence, brewing goes like this: crushed malt meets water and goes through mashing to turn starch into sugar; the wort is separated, boiled, and hopped according to the recipe; then it’s cooled, pitched with yeast, fermented, matured, and packaged before it reaches our table.

An easy trick for remembering hops is looking at when they go in. Early in the boil mostly builds bitterness; later additions lean toward aroma. Dry hopping, which shows up on tap lists all the time, means adding hops during or after fermentation to push the aroma front and center. Still, the term only tells you the timing—whether it smells like citrus or pine depends on the hop varieties in the recipe. 2

First, Distinguish Ale from Lager#

The first fork in the road on any beer menu is ale versus lager. The main difference comes down to fermentation: ale yeast generally works warmer, while lager yeast ferments colder and usually gets a stretch of cold maturation afterward. Craft brewing loves exceptions, of course, so if something breaks the mold, check the yeast and process behind it.

If you don’t want to memorize definitions, it’s faster to anchor a few familiar examples: wheat beers, IPAs, porters, and stouts sit on the ale side, while pilsners, Munich Helles, dark lagers, and bocks are lagers. Neither side is automatically light or heavy—both span the full range. As for the “top-fermenting” and “bottom-fermenting” labels, those are fun to look up if you’re curious about how yeast actually behaves in the tank. 3

Decoding the Label: Common Terms#

Reading the Numbers#

LabelMeaningHow It Helps When Ordering
ABV (Alcohol by Volume)Alcohol by volume5% ABV means roughly 5% alcohol by volume—combine that with how much you drink to gauge total intake
IBU (International Bitterness Units)International bitterness unitsMeasures bitter compounds, not exact perceived bitterness—sweetness and body can soften the bite
OG / FG (Original / Final Gravity)Original / final gravitySpecific gravity of wort before fermentation and beer afterward; mostly used for brewing logs and estimating ABV
°P (Degrees Plato)Degrees PlatoOften listed as original wort concentration on labels, measuring extract before fermentation—not alcohol content
SRM / EBCTwo color scalesHigher numbers generally mean darker beer; the two scales aren’t interchangeable, and dark color doesn’t mean high ABV

Standing in front of a fridge or tap list, I usually scan three things first: style, ABV, and packaging date. I treat IBU as a rough hint of bitterness, since how it actually feels still comes down to tasting it. 2

Reading Flavor and Process Terms#

  • Body: How light or full the beer feels in your mouth—texture, not color.
  • Dry: Low sweetness and a crisp finish; it doesn’t mean bitter, nor completely sugar-free.
  • Esters and phenols: In tasting notes, esters tend to suggest fruit while phenols lean toward spices like clove; whether they fit depends on the style.
  • Carbonation: The prickly fizz from carbon dioxide, separate from body or smoothness.
  • Finish / aftertaste: What lingers after you swallow—roast, bitterness, or a clean, dry snap.
  • Barrel-aged: Matured in wooden barrels, often picking up oak or notes from whatever spirit the barrel held before; it’s a process rather than a standalone base style.
  • Bottle-conditioned: Continues fermenting in the bottle after packaging, usually for natural carbonation, so a little yeast sediment at the bottom is completely normal. 2

A few prefixes and abbreviations pop up in beer names all the time, so they’re worth keeping handy: Session usually means a lower-ABV, easy-drinking version; Double or Imperial signals a beefed-up take, though ABV still tells the real story; DDH stands for “double dry hopped,” and how many additions or how much hops that actually means varies by brewery. 24

Common Styles: How Do They Taste Different?#

Let’s walk through the menu the way I would with a friend, grouping the styles you’re most likely to run into by how they generally drink. Even within the same style, of course, two breweries can take things in very different directions.

Lagers and Pilsners: Crisp with Layers#

The lager you’ll spot most often is probably Pilsner (or Pils)—crisp and refreshing, with noticeably more hop bitterness, herbal lift, and floral aroma than standard macro lager. If Munich pale lager (Helles) is sitting next to it, expect a softer, rounder malt profile. And lagers aren’t all pale either—dark lagers exist too, so going by style name beats judging by color.

I actually slow down with lighter beers like these, paying attention to how cleanly they finish and whether malt sweetness or hop bitterness lingers longer. Light doesn’t mean simple; a lot of the good stuff only shows up when you take your time. 3

Wheat Beers: German and Belgian Styles Differ#

Mention German wheat beer (Weissbier / Hefeweizen) and the first things that pop into my head are banana and clove aromas, a pillowy head of foam, and low bitterness. Don’t be surprised by the banana note—it’s usually not added fruit, just the yeast doing its thing during fermentation. 5

Belgian Witbier uses wheat too, but heads in a different direction—often brewed with coriander seed and orange peel, so it drinks bright and refreshing with citrus and gentle spice. The “white” in the name is mostly a historical style term, so don’t expect a glass of milk. 6

If you’re ready for a break from lager without jumping straight into heavy bitterness, German wheat beer and Witbier are both easy next steps—just pick between banana-clove and citrus-spice.

Pale Ale: A Middle Ground for Getting to Know Hops#

When I think of pale ale, American Pale Ale (APA) is usually what comes to mind. The hop aroma is already clear, the malt backbone is still there, and compared with an aggressive American IPA it tends to be much more approachable.

If you want to test whether you enjoy hop-forward beers, starting with a pale ale is a solid move. And just as a side note, “pale” was coined relative to older dark beers, so the color can run anywhere from light gold to amber. 7

IPA: Not Just One Kind of “Bitter Beer”#

IPA stands for India Pale Ale, easily the three letters with the loudest presence on most craft tap lists. These days IPA splits into quite a few directions, so it pays to look at whatever modifier sits in front of it.

American / West Coast IPA puts hops right up front—think citrus, pine, and floral notes. West Coast styles in particular finish dry with a sharp, clean bitterness. If you like a grapefruit-peel bite, this is where to look. 7

Hazy IPA / New England IPA (NEIPA) goes a different way: big tropical-fruit hop aromas, a softer and fuller mouthfeel, and far less perceived bitterness than a classic IPA. Plenty of them look straight-up like juice, though whether any real fruit went in—and how strong they are—still lives on the ingredient list and ABV line, so don’t chug them like soda. 8

When you spot Double IPA (DIPA) / Imperial IPA, both strength and hop intensity jump a notch. Even if regular IPAs go down smoothly for you, these call for a slower pace—the body and kick are a whole different ballgame. 3

So at the bar, telling the bartender “I want plenty of hop aroma, not too bitter, and not too strong” usually gets you much closer to what you want than simply asking for “an IPA.”

Porters and Stouts: Coffee, Chocolate, and Roasted Notes#

I mentioned earlier that stout is my favorite, and porter is usually mentioned in the same breath. Both lean into coffee, cocoa, and roasted grain notes; modern recipes overlap so much that instead of stressing over the label, I’d rather just remember which brewery’s version I liked. 3

Plenty of people see a pitch-black beer and assume it’s heavy and boozy, yet a classic Irish stout can pack plenty of roast, finish crisp and dry, and sit at a surprisingly modest ABV. Don’t let the dark color and thick foam intimidate you—check the ABV and taste how sweet or dry it actually is. 9

Another branch of stout adds lactose, coffee, cocoa nibs, or vanilla, heading into richer, dessert-like territory. Just check the ingredients before ordering a Milk Stout—standard brewing yeast doesn’t fully ferment lactose, which is worth watching out for if you’re lactose intolerant. 2

If you already love coffee or dark chocolate, following those roasted notes into porters and stouts is usually a safe bet. Deciding ahead of time whether you want something dry and crisp or rich and dessert-like makes a huge difference.

Belgian Styles: Let the Yeast Take Center Stage#

One of the more fun Belgian styles to know is Tripel. It often pours bright gold and looks harmless enough, yet packs a serious ABV; the fruity, spicy yeast character paired with a dry finish makes it hard to forget. “Tripel” is just the style name, not triple the alcohol—check the ABV for the real number. 10

Beyond that, Dubbel brings darker color and deeper caramel-malt notes, while Saison tends to finish dry and peppery with a distinctive fermentation aroma. Keeping them as separate styles makes navigating a menu much easier. 3

Drink enough Belgian beers and you start realizing that when fruit or spice hits your nose, yeast is often the one pulling the strings.

Sour Beers and Fruit Beers: Separate Tartness, Sweetness, and Fruit#

I don’t order sour beer very often myself, though there’s a wide world of flavors inside it. Gose, for instance, pairs clean tartness with a restrained touch of salt and coriander. If a menu only says “sour,” asking for the exact style helps a lot. 11

With fruit beers, I usually check two things first: what the base style is, and whether it leans tart or sweet. “Fruit purée” or “smoothie” beers—loaded with purée and poured thick—are everywhere now, though every brewery names them slightly differently. 3

So even if you’re ordering for a favorite fruit, ask how tart, sweet, light, or thick it drinks—giving only a fruit name can land you a glass completely different from what you pictured.

How to Order at a Craft Beer Bar for the First Time#

After all that style talk, when I actually sit down with a friend I’m not reciting a textbook—I usually just ask, “What are you in the mood for tonight?” Something crisp and refreshing, hoppy with a bit of bite, or rich and full? This cheat sheet is handy when you’re staring down a tap list:

What You Feel LikeStyles to Look AtOne Extra Thing to Ask
Crisp, clean, not too heavyHelles, pilsnerLow bitterness, or okay with a noticeable bite?
Aromatic, without much bitternessGerman wheat beer, Belgian witbierBanana and clove, or citrus and spice?
Curious about hop aromaAPA, hazy IPAPrefer lower ABV? Also ask about the bitterness
Dry with a clear, crisp bitternessWest Coast IPAHow much bitterness and alcohol warmth do you want?
Coffee or cocoa notesPorter, stoutDry and crisp, or sweet and dessert-like?
Tartness or fruit flavorsSour beer, fruit beerCheck tartness, sweetness, and whether it’s thick purée

If the bar offers small pours or a tasting flight, picking two or three contrasting styles side by side is a fun way to explore. Running into one you dislike isn’t a loss either—make a mental note and skip it next time. On a first visit, ordering whatever sounds appealing and enjoying it slowly is more than enough.

I can rarely keep track of elaborate beer names anyway, so I just note the style, ABV, and my gut reaction—something like “bright citrus, too bitter at the end.” Next time I order, that one line helps way more than trying to memorize the name.

How to Drink to Better Distinguish Flavors#

If you want to taste a bit more closely, you don’t need a whole ceremony. Grab a clean glass with no detergent residue, pour a little in, look, smell, and pause for a few seconds after a sip—the layers tend to show themselves:

  1. Look: Check the color, clarity, and foam. They hint at the style, not whether the beer is good.
  2. Smell: Catch the first impression that jumps out—bread, citrus, banana, or coffee? No need to force ten adjectives.
  3. Taste: Notice sweetness, bitterness, and acidity alongside body, carbonation, and alcohol warmth.
  4. Pause: Pay attention to the finish and aftertaste, then ask yourself one simple question: do I want another sip?

In my experience, when a beer is ice-cold, the aroma tends to hide. Crisp styles are great cold, while heavier beers like stout open up nicely if I let them sit and warm slightly before the next sip. When in doubt, start with the brewery’s serving suggestion and trust your own palate from there. 2

And don’t stress about tasting vocabulary. Saying “like grapefruit peel,” “too bitter at the end,” or “kind of like toast” works just fine—when chatting with friends, saying what you actually tasted is all that matters.

Storage and Common Misconceptions#

When I bring beer home, I check the storage note on the back label first. If a fruit purée beer says keep cold, into the fridge it goes; with hop-forward beers (especially IPAs), I pay extra attention to freshness too. Keep bottles and cans away from heat and direct sunlight, and don’t assume every beer improves with age—only certain styles are built for cellaring. 2

To wrap up the reference part, here are a few things that are surprisingly easy to mix up when reading labels:

  • “12° original wort means 12% alcohol.” Completely different metrics—always check ABV for alcohol content.
  • “Double the IBU means twice as bitter.” Sweetness, body, and balance all soften perceived bitterness, so the number alone doesn’t tell the whole story.
  • “Banana aroma means bananas were added.” In German wheat beers, that classic banana note comes from yeast fermentation.
  • “Dark beer is always strong.” Dark malts drive color and roasted flavor; ABV is what tells you the strength.
  • “The hazier, the better.” Some styles are supposed to be hazy and others crystal clear—judge within the style.
  • “If it tastes like fruit juice, I can drink as much as I want.” Going down easy doesn’t erase the alcohol; ABV and volume still count.

One quick note: this whole post is about flavor and enjoyment, not health advice. Never drive after drinking—and if you don’t drink at all, there’s still nothing stopping you from reading this as fun trivia.

Conclusion#

Writing all this brings me right back to those evenings trying to figure out where to meet friends. After going back and forth, I still love settling into a comfortable craft beer bar with a glass I enjoy, talking, or playing a quick game. One beer taken slowly, and the whole evening slips by without any rush.
