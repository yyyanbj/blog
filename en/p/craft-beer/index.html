Preface#

If you asked me to pick one kind of drink, I’d probably still say craft beer.

What I like is how many different flavors I can have in a glass. A light beer goes down easily with food. Some cocktails are stronger, and if I nurse an iced drink it gets diluted, while drinking it quickly makes it easy to lose track. With craft beer I can see whether I’m in the mood for malt, the roast I associate with coffee or cocoa, or something bitter, tart, sweet, or fruity. It still depends on what the bar has.

I usually spend 30–50 minutes on a glass. Going slowly lets me notice what I’m tasting. There is a point where I’ve let it sit too long, too. Once it has warmed up and lost some bubbles, it can feel different from the beer I liked when I first picked it up.

If I put them in order, I’d start with a rich stout, then IPA, lagers and wheat beers, with the fruit purée ones further down. I never quite got used to sour beer. It reminds me a little of red wine, which also happens to be a taste I don’t get on with.

There’s something else I enjoy besides the beer. I can sit with friends and talk, and take my time over one glass. Sitting at the bar feels different. Sometimes a conversation starts and I get to meet someone interesting.

Beer talk is always welcome. 🍺

I also wanted to talk through the words I keep seeing on beer menus. I used GPT to help organize the notes on ingredients and styles, so I can find them here again later.

What Exactly Is Craft Beer?#

I start with how the words craft beer get used. It turns up on all sorts of styles and the definition varies by place. For its statistics, the Brewers Association defines American craft brewers by size and independence. I keep in mind which breweries that definition was meant to cover. 1

In conversation it mostly makes me think of what I can taste in the glass. Change a malt, hop, or yeast, add fruit or spice, or age it in a barrel and the flavor may change. There are a few things I’d look at a little closer on a menu.

  • IPA is easy to spot on a menu. Look beside it and the same craft brewery may have a lager, a wheat beer, and other traditional styles.
  • When I want something refreshing, I look for a beer with less bitterness or lower ABV.
  • Haziness, filtration, and adjuncts tell me something about how it was made. A clear beer can taste good, and well-chosen adjuncts can contribute their own flavors.
  • I glance at the label and the price, then find out whether the beer suits me from what’s in the glass.

With a beer I haven’t tried, I usually start by asking the style and how fresh it is. After a sip I can think about how the flavors go together and whether I want another.

Where Does a Beer’s Flavor Come From?#

Four Basic Ingredients#

To find where a flavor might come from, start with the four usual ingredients, water, malt, hops, and yeast. There may be unmalted grains, sugar, fruit, or spices too. That depends on what went into the particular beer.

IngredientPrimary RoleFlavor or Mouthfeel to Watch For
WaterProvides the brewing base; mineral composition affects brewing and flavor expressionThe same recipe can yield different levels of smoothness and bitterness
MaltProvides starch for mashing and influences color and flavorGrain, bread, biscuit, caramel; darker malts may also impart coffee or roasted notes
HopsProvide bitterness, as well as various aromas and flavorsFloral, herbal, citrus, pine, or tropical fruit notes
YeastConverts fermentable sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide, and creates fermentation flavorsSome yeasts impart fruity or spicy notes, while others produce cleaner profiles

When something tastes a lot like fruit or coffee, I turn to the ingredient list. Fermentation or roasting can give that aroma. The list tells me whether fruit or coffee actually went in. 2

A Simplified Look at Brewing#

Follow the beer through the brewery. Crushed malt goes in water and mashing turns its starch into sugar. Separate the wort and boil it, adding hops where the recipe calls for them. Then it cools, gets yeast, ferments, matures, and goes into the package before it gets to us.

With hops I look at when they went in. Add them during the boil for bitterness, or later for more aroma. Dry hopping is adding hops during or after fermentation, mainly to bring up the aroma. The name tells you when they went in. Look at the recipe for what hops were used. 2

First, Distinguish Ale from Lager#

When I see ale and lager, I first think about fermentation. Ale yeast generally works at a warmer temperature. Lager yeast is usually fermented colder, followed by cold maturation. For an exception I’d go back and see what yeast and process it used.

I keep a few menu examples in mind. Wheat beer, IPA, porter, and stout are on the ale side. Pilsner, Munich pale lager, dark lager, and bock are lagers. There are light and fuller beers on either side. If the names top- and bottom-fermenting make you curious, look at what the yeast does over the course of fermentation. 3

Decoding the Label: Common Terms#

Reading the numbers#

LabelMeaningHow it helps when ordering
ABV (Alcohol by Volume)Alcohol by volumeA beer at 5% ABV is about 5% alcohol by volume. Together with the amount you drink, this determines how much alcohol you consume
IBU (International Bitterness Units)International bitterness unitsIndicates the level of bitter compounds, but not exactly how bitter a beer tastes; sweetness and body also affect perception
OG / FG (Original / Final Gravity)Original / final gravityThe specific gravity of wort before fermentation and beer afterward, used in brewing records and estimating alcohol content
°P (Degrees Plato)Degrees PlatoOften used for original wort concentration on a label, indicating the extract in wort before fermentation; it is not alcohol content
SRM / EBCTwo color scalesHigher values usually mean a darker beer. The scales cannot be used interchangeably, and a darker color does not mean more alcohol

At the counter I’d read the style, ABV, and packaging date first. IBU gives me a clue about bitterness. Whether I like the beer is something I’ll find out from a sip. 2

Reading flavor and process terms#

  • Body: How light or full the beer feels in your mouth; it is not the same as color.
  • Dry: Usually describes less sweetness and a crisp finish. It does not mean bitter, or necessarily mean sugar-free.
  • Esters and phenols: Esters often suggest fruit, while phenols can suggest spices such as clove. Whether those notes fit depends on the style.
  • Carbonation: The tingling sensation from carbon dioxide, distinct from body or smoothness.
  • Finish / aftertaste: What remains after swallowing, such as roasted notes, bitterness, or dryness.
  • Barrel-aged: Matured in a wooden barrel, which can add wood flavors or flavors from whatever the barrel previously held. It is not a separate base style.
  • Bottle-conditioned: Fermentation continues in the bottle after packaging, usually to create carbonation, and may leave yeast sediment. 2

There are a few words on beer names that send me back to the label. Session usually means lower alcohol and easier drinking. Double or Imperial generally means a stronger version, and the actual strength is in the ABV. DDH is double dry hopped. For the number of additions or how much went in, check how the brewery uses the name. 4 2

Common styles: How do they taste different?#

Let’s go through the menu as if we were sitting there together. I’ve grouped a few of the styles you’ll run into by how they tend to drink. Breweries can make quite different beers within the same style.

Lagers and Pilsners: Crisp with layers#

One lager I keep seeing is Pilsner. It’s refreshing, and the hop bitterness and herbal or floral notes are usually easier to pick out. Munich pale lager, or Helles, can give a softer malt taste alongside it. There are dark lagers too, so I start with the style name.

I let this kind sit a moment and see how cleanly it finishes, and whether the malt or bitterness stays longer. I like some lighter beers for those smaller details that show up when I slow down. 3

Wheat beers: German and Belgian styles differ#

For German wheat beer, Weissbier or Hefeweizen, I think first of banana and clove, lots of foam, and fairly low bitterness. If you smell banana, think about the yeast. Fermentation commonly brings out that flavor. 5

Look a little farther for Belgian Witbier and there’s wheat there too. Coriander and orange peel often go along with it, so it drinks fresh with citrus and spice. White is the style name. Look at the actual color when it arrives. 6

If you’ve had enough lager for the moment and want something light on bitterness, have a look at German wheat beer or Witbier. Check which one the bar has and pick the aromas you’re in the mood for.

Pale ale: A first step toward understanding hops#

For pale ale I think of a typical American pale ale, or APA. The hops are there to taste and I can still pick out malt. Next to a typical American IPA, the APA is often a little more restrained.

If you want to get a feel for hop aroma, a pale ale is a place to start. Pale is relative to the older dark beers. When it arrives you can see how pale this brewery’s version actually is. 7

IPA: Not just one kind of “bitter beer”#

IPA is all over beer menus and stands for India Pale Ale. When I’m about to order one I look at what else the name says. The variations we get now can taste quite different.

With an American or West Coast IPA, hops often stand out with citrus, pine, or flowers. A typical West Coast example finishes drier and has a clearer bitterness. If you like a grapefruit-peel bite and a clean finish, look in this direction. 7

A hazy IPA or New England IPA often gives me tropical-fruit hop aromas and a softer, fuller feel, with less perceived bitterness than a traditional IPA. It can look like juice. I check the ingredients for actual juice and the ABV for strength. Whether it suits me still comes down to the sip. 8

With a double or imperial IPA, the alcohol and hop expression usually go up. If regular IPA goes down easily, notice the alcohol and body in this one before settling into the same pace. 3

When ordering, “I’d like plenty of hop aroma, but not too much bitterness or alcohol” is often more helpful than simply asking for an IPA.

Porters and Stouts: Coffee, Chocolate, and Roasted Notes#

I mentioned liking stout earlier. Porter is another dark style that often has coffee, cocoa, or roast, just as stout does. Modern recipes overlap, so I remember which brewery and beer I’m tasting as well as what style name they used. 3

Take an Irish stout. It can have a good amount of roast, a dry finish, and a fairly low ABV. If it comes out dark with a thick head, I check the ABV and see for myself how sweet it tastes. 9

Other stouts use ingredients such as lactose, coffee, cocoa, or vanilla for a sweeter, more dessert-like profile. When you see a milk stout, check the ingredients: ordinary brewing yeast may not fully ferment lactose, which matters especially to people with lactose intolerance. 2

If I want something like coffee or dark chocolate, I look for roast. Mention whether you’d like it to finish dry or want a fuller beer that feels like dessert. It makes quite a difference to what you’ll get.

Belgian Styles: Let the Yeast Take Center Stage#

Take Tripel from the Belgian styles. It’s often pale and fairly strong. I tend to notice the fruit and spice from fermentation and the dry finish as well. Tripel is the style name. For strength I still check ABV. 10

There’s also Dubbel, with a darker malt character, and Saison, which usually finishes drier and has its own fermentation aromas. I remember them as separate styles and can find them more easily that way. 3

So the next time I smell fruit or spice, I’ll think of the yeast too. It may be what made that aroma.

Sour Beers and Fruit Beers: Distinguish Sour, Sweet, and Fruity Notes#

I drink less sour beer myself, and there’s quite a range of flavors in it. Gose, for example, can bring some restrained salt and coriander alongside the tartness. If the menu only says sour, look for which style the beer actually is. 11

For a fruit beer I ask about the base style and whether the result is tart or sweeter. Fruit purée or smoothie often describes a fuller beer with quite a bit of purée. The names vary from one brewery to another. 3

If there’s a particular fruit you like, ask a couple more things. How tart or sweet is it? Does it drink light or thick like purée? Giving only the fruit name can get you a glass quite different from the one you had in mind.

How to Order at a Craft Beer Bar for the First Time#

After all the style names, when I sit down with a friend I usually ask what they feel like tasting. Something refreshing, something with hop bitterness, or something fuller? The options below are a way to look at the menu and see what sounds right.

What you would likeStyles to exploreWhat to clarify when ordering
Crisp and clean, not too intenseHelles, pilsnerDo you prefer low bitterness, or are you comfortable with a more noticeable bitterness?
Aromatic, but not too bitterGerman wheat beer, Belgian witbierDo you prefer banana and clove, or citrus and spice?
Hop aromaAPA, hazy IPAAsk for lower alcohol if preferred, and check the bitterness
Dry with distinct bitternessWest Coast IPAHow much bitterness and alcohol warmth do you enjoy?
Coffee or cocoaPorter, stoutWould you prefer dry, or sweet and full-bodied?
Sourness or fruitSour beer, fruit beerCheck the tartness, sweetness, and purée-like texture

If there are small pours or a flight, try a few that differ enough to compare. If one doesn’t suit you, make a note and move on. On a first visit it’s fine just to order the glass you want.

I sometimes forget the name and write down the style, the ABV, and what stayed with me. Citrus up front, too bitter at the end is plenty. I’ll know what to ask for next time.

How to Drink to Better Distinguish Flavors#

A clean glass without detergent is enough for me. Pour in a little, have a look, then smell it. After a sip, give it a moment and see which flavors stick around.

  1. Look: Notice the color, clarity, and foam. They help you understand the style but cannot determine quality on their own.
  2. Smell: Start with your strongest impression. Is it bread, citrus, banana, or coffee? You do not need ten adjectives at once.
  3. Taste: Notice the sweetness, bitterness, acidity, body, carbonation, and alcohol.
  4. Wait: Pay attention to the finish and aftertaste, then decide whether you want another sip.

When a beer is very cold, I often struggle to pick out the aroma. I can drink a refreshing one colder, and I’ll let a fuller beer sit for a bit before another sip. When in doubt, I start with the brewery’s serving advice and see how it tastes to me. 2

If all I have for a sip is “grapefruit peel,” “too bitter at the end,” or “a little like toast,” that’s good enough for me. When we talk about it later we can just say what we tasted.

Storage and Common Misconceptions#

At home I start with the storage instructions on the back. If a fruit purée beer needs refrigeration, follow what the brewery says. I also pay attention to freshness with hoppy beer. Keep it away from heat and direct sun, and check whether the particular beer is suitable for aging. 2

Here are a few things I’ve written down for the next time the label leaves me confused.

  • “A 12° original wort concentration means 12% alcohol.” These are different measures; look at ABV for alcohol content.
  • “Twice the IBU means it will taste twice as bitter.” Perceived bitterness is also affected by sweetness, body, and overall balance.
  • “A banana aroma means bananas were added.” The characteristic banana note in German wheat beer can come from yeast fermentation.
  • “Dark beer is always high in alcohol.” Dark ingredients affect color, but only the alcohol label tells you the ABV.
  • “The cloudier, the better.” Some styles are meant to be hazy and others clear; judge them in the context of the style.
  • “If it tastes fruity like a soft drink, I can drink as much as I like.” Easy drinking does not change the alcohol content; pay attention to ABV and how much you consume.

This article discusses flavor and the experience of drinking, not drinking as health advice. Do not drive after drinking; you can understand these ideas without drinking at all.

Conclusion#

This brings me back to making plans with friends and going back and forth about where to meet. I’m still happy sitting down in a craft beer bar with a glass I want, talking or playing a game. I take my time over it and the time passes easily.
