Preface#

Craft beer is my favorite kind of alcoholic drink and has an important place in my life.

What draws me in is its range of flavors. Light, “watery” beer is often treated as a drink to have with a meal, while some cocktails have a higher alcohol content: if you sip an iced cocktail too slowly, the melting ice can dilute its flavor; drink it too quickly, and it is easy to lose track of how much you have had. Craft beer offers plenty of choices, from fresh malt, roasted coffee and cocoa, and hoppy bitterness to sweet-and-sour or fruity flavors. There is a good chance of finding something you enjoy, though the options depend on the bar’s selection.

I usually like to take 30–50 minutes over a glass. Drink too quickly and I might miss the details; leave it too long and changes in temperature and carbonation may take it past the point I enjoy most.

Rich stouts are my favorite, followed by IPAs, then lagers and wheat beers, with fruit purée beers further down the list. Sour beers are harder for me to enjoy: they tend to remind me of red wine, which is not a flavor I particularly like.

Craft beer bars often have a relaxed atmosphere, making them good places to chat with friends. Sitting at the bar offers a different experience and a chance to meet interesting people. That, too, is part of the appeal for me.

Beer talk is always welcome. 🍺

The rest of this post shares some craft beer basics I put together with help from GPT.

What Exactly Is Craft Beer?#

The Chinese term jingniang corresponds to “craft beer,” but it is not a specific beer style, and there is no single definition used around the world. The Brewers Association’s definition applies to American craft breweries and emphasizes small scale and independent ownership. It is an industry definition used for statistical purposes, not a standard that can be applied directly to every beer in every country. 1

In everyday conversation, “craft” often suggests an exploration of flavors and recipes: different malts, hops, and yeasts, as well as variations introduced by fruit, spices, or barrel aging. But none of the following is a reliable rule:

  • Craft does not mean IPA. Craft breweries also make lagers, wheat beers, and other traditional styles.
  • Craft does not have to mean bitter, intense, or high in alcohol. Refreshing beers with low bitterness can be just as interesting.
  • Haze, lack of filtration, or the absence of additives is not enough to define craft beer. Clear beers can taste good, and using adjuncts appropriately does not imply poor quality.
  • Price and packaging cannot judge flavor for you. However attractive the label, what matters is the beer in the glass.

Rather than arguing over whether a bottle is “craft” enough, ask: What style is it? Is it fresh? Are the flavors balanced? Do I like it?

Where Does a Beer’s Flavor Come From?#

Four Basic Ingredients#

Beer can be understood through four basic ingredients: water, malt, hops, and yeast. Unmalted grains, sugar, fruit, and spices may also be used.

IngredientPrimary RoleFlavor or Mouthfeel to Watch For
WaterProvides the brewing base; mineral composition affects brewing and flavor expressionThe same recipe can yield different levels of smoothness and bitterness
MaltProvides starch for mashing and influences color and flavorGrain, bread, biscuit, caramel; darker malts may also impart coffee or roasted notes
HopsProvide bitterness, as well as various aromas and flavorsFloral, herbal, citrus, pine, or tropical fruit notes
YeastConverts fermentable sugars into alcohol and carbon dioxide, and creates fermentation flavorsSome yeasts impart fruity or spicy notes, while others produce cleaner profiles

That means a fruity aroma does not necessarily mean fruit was added, and a coffee flavor does not necessarily mean coffee was added. Check the ingredient list to see whether those ingredients are actually present. 2

A Simplified Look at Brewing#

Crushed malt is mixed with water and mashed to convert its starches into sugars. The wort is separated and boiled, with hops added according to the recipe. It is then cooled, pitched with yeast, fermented, conditioned, and packaged to become the beer we drink.

Hops contribute different things depending on when they are added. Additions during the boil help develop bitterness, while later additions place more emphasis on aroma. Dry hopping means adding hops during or after fermentation, mainly to enhance hop aroma; it does not mean drying the hops before adding them. 2

First, Distinguish Ale from Lager#

Ale and lager are primarily fermentation categories, not grades of quality. As a starting point, ales generally use ale yeast at warmer fermentation temperatures, while lagers generally use lager yeast at cooler temperatures and are cold-conditioned. There are exceptions in practice, so temperature or flavor alone cannot settle the question.

Ales include wheat beers, IPAs, porters, and stouts; lagers include pilsners, Munich helles, dark lagers, and bocks. Ales are not necessarily heavy, and lagers are not necessarily bland. “Top-fermenting” and “bottom-fermenting” are common terms, but they do not mean yeast works only in one layer of the beer. 3

Decoding the Label: Common Terms#

Reading the numbers#

ABV (Alcohol by Volume)Alcohol by volumeA beer at 5% ABV is about 5% alcohol by volume. Together with the amount you drink, this determines how much alcohol you consume
IBU (International Bitterness Units)International bitterness unitsIndicates the level of bitter compounds, but not exactly how bitter a beer tastes; sweetness and body also affect perception
OG / FG (Original / Final Gravity)Original / final gravityThe specific gravity of wort before fermentation and beer afterward, used in brewing records and estimating alcohol content

For most drinkers, the style, ABV, and packaging date are enough to start with. IBU can help inform a choice, but there is no need to treat it as a ranking of flavor. 2

Reading flavor and process terms#

  • Body: How light or full the beer feels in your mouth; it is not the same as color.
  • Dry: Usually describes less sweetness and a crisp finish. It does not mean bitter, or necessarily mean sugar-free.
  • Esters and phenols: Esters often suggest fruit, while phenols can suggest spices such as clove. Whether those notes fit depends on the style.
  • Carbonation: The tingling sensation from carbon dioxide, distinct from body or smoothness.
  • Finish / aftertaste: What remains after swallowing, such as roasted notes, bitterness, or dryness.
  • Barrel-aged: Matured in a wooden barrel, which can add wood flavors or flavors from whatever the barrel previously held. It is not a separate base style.
  • Bottle-conditioned: Fermentation continues in the bottle after packaging, usually to create carbonation, and may leave yeast sediment. 2

Other terms often appear in beer names. Session usually emphasizes lower alcohol and easy drinking; Double / Imperial generally indicates a stronger version; and DDH (Double Dry Hopped) refers to double dry hopping, although the number of additions, quantities, and naming conventions vary by brewery. These names are not a universal scale: in particular, “Double” does not mean the ABV is exactly twice as high. 4 2

Common styles: How do they taste different?#

The following styles are grouped by their typical flavors. Think of these descriptions as signposts rather than strict templates: recipes can vary considerably even within one style.

Lagers and Pilsners: Crisp with layers#

Lager is a broad category, and pilsner (Pilsner / Pils) is one style within it. Pilsners are often crisp, with more distinct hop bitterness and herbal or floral notes; Munich helles tends toward a softer malt character. Dark lagers exist too, so color alone cannot tell you whether a beer is a lager.

Notice whether the malt and bitterness are balanced and whether the finish is clean. A “light” beer may have understated flavors without being any less skillfully brewed. 3

Wheat beers: German and Belgian styles differ#

German wheat beer (Weissbier / Hefeweizen) often has banana- and clove-like fermentation aromas, a generous head, and low bitterness. The banana note usually comes from the yeast; it does not mean bananas were added. 5

Belgian witbier also uses wheat, often alongside ingredients such as coriander seed and orange peel. It tends to be refreshing, with citrus and spice notes. The “white” in its name describes the style, not the literal color of the beer. 6

If ordinary lager feels a little monotonous but you do not want strong bitterness, both are worth exploring. When ordering, specifying German wheat beer or Belgian witbier is more precise than simply asking for a wheat beer.

Pale ale: A first step toward understanding hops#

Pale ale is not a fixed recipe. American pale ale (APA) often emphasizes hop aroma while retaining malt support; compared with American IPA, it is usually more restrained in strength and hop character.

If you want to explore hop aroma but worry that IPA might be too intense, pale ale can be a place to start. “Pale” is relative to traditional dark beers; it does not guarantee that every glass will be especially light in color. 7

IPA: Not just one kind of “bitter beer”#

IPA stands for India Pale Ale. It has since branched into many styles, so those three letters alone cannot tell you exactly what the beer will taste like.

American IPA / West Coast IPA: Hop-forward, with possible notes of citrus, pine, and flowers. West Coast versions are typically drier, with more pronounced bitterness. If you like grapefruit-like bitterness and a crisp finish, they are worth exploring. 7

Hazy IPA / New England IPA (NEIPA): These emphasize tropical fruit-like hop aromas and a soft, full mouthfeel, with perceived bitterness usually more restrained than in traditional IPAs. A “juicy” character does not mean juice was added, or that the beer has no alcohol or bitterness. The amount of haze is not a measure of quality, either. 8

Double IPA (DIPA) / Imperial IPA: Usually higher in alcohol and more intense in hop character. Enjoying a regular IPA does not mean you will necessarily prefer a stronger version: the alcohol, body, and pace of drinking all change. 3

When ordering, “I’d like plenty of hop aroma, but not too much bitterness or alcohol” is often more helpful than simply asking for an IPA.

Porters and Stouts: Coffee, Chocolate, and Roasted Notes#

Porters and stouts are both families of dark beers, often with roasted, coffee, or cocoa-like flavors. Modern recipes overlap, so you cannot draw an absolute line between them based on color or a single ingredient. 3

An Irish stout, for example, can have a pronounced roasted character and a relatively dry finish without being high in alcohol. Black does not necessarily mean strong, and a thick head does not necessarily mean sweet. 9

Other stouts use ingredients such as lactose, coffee, cocoa, or vanilla for a sweeter, more dessert-like profile. When you see a milk stout, check the ingredients: ordinary brewing yeast may not fully ferment lactose, which matters especially to people with lactose intolerance. 2

If you enjoy coffee or dark chocolate, roasted beers are a good place to explore. Consider whether you prefer dry roasted notes or a richer, dessert-like mouthfeel.

Belgian Styles: Let the Yeast Take Center Stage#

Belgian styles do not share a single flavor profile. A tripel, for example, is usually pale but relatively high in alcohol, with fruity and spicy fermentation notes and a fairly dry finish. The “three” in its name does not mean three fermentations or three times the alcohol. 10

Other familiar styles include dubbel, with its darker malt character, and saison, which tends to be dry and aromatic from fermentation. These are distinct traditional styles, not different sizes or strength levels of the same beer. 3

These beers remind us that aroma does not come only from hops. Fruit and spice notes can also lead us to consider the yeast.

Sour Beers and Fruit Beers: Distinguish Sour, Sweet, and Fruity Notes#

Sour beers are a group with varied flavors and brewing methods, not a single recipe. Gose, for example, typically combines tartness with restrained salt and coriander notes; it is not simply “fruit soda with salt.” 11

Fruit beers can be built on different base styles and may or may not be sour. A “fruit sour” on a menu is not necessarily the same as a “fruit purée” or “smoothie” beer: the latter often contains more fruit purée and has a thicker texture, though breweries do not use these terms uniformly. 3

If you enjoy fruit flavors, ask how tart or sweet the beer is and whether its texture is light or thick. Naming a favorite fruit alone could still get you something quite different from what you expected.

How to Order at a Craft Beer Bar for the First Time#

Start by describing what you like, then choose a style. The suggestions below follow the flavor profiles above; they are starting points, not guaranteed matches for everyone:

Coffee or cocoaPorter, stoutWould you prefer dry, or sweet and full-bodied?

If the bar offers small pours or tasting flights, choose a few noticeably different styles to compare. There is no need to try every style on your first visit, or to force yourself to accept a flavor you dislike just because you are a beginner.

More useful than memorizing names is noting three things: the style, ABV, and why you liked or disliked the beer. “I liked the citrus aroma, but the finish was too bitter,” for example, gives you a clearer direction next time.

How to Drink to Better Distinguish Flavors#

There is no need for an elaborate ritual. Pour a reasonable amount into a clean glass without detergent residue, then follow these steps:

  1. Look: Notice the color, clarity, and foam. They help you understand the style but cannot determine quality on their own.
  2. Smell: Start with your strongest impression. Is it bread, citrus, banana, or coffee? You do not need ten adjectives at once.
  3. Taste: Notice the sweetness, bitterness, acidity, body, carbonation, and alcohol.
  4. Wait: Pay attention to the finish and aftertaste, then decide whether you want another sip.

Temperature changes the experience too. When beer is too cold, aromas and flavors can be harder to distinguish. Crisp beers can be served cooler, while richer beers invite comparison as they warm naturally in the glass. Follow the brewery’s serving suggestions first; there is no need to seek one “standard temperature” for every beer. 2

There is no single right way to describe a flavor. “This reminds me of grapefruit peel,” “the aftertaste is too bitter,” or “this tastes like toast” is more useful than forcing a string of technical terms.

Storage and Common Misconceptions#

Once you bring beer home, check the label’s storage instructions, especially if it needs refrigeration. Freshness is particularly worth watching for beers with prominent hop aroma, and refrigerated fruit purée beers should be stored as the brewery directs. Avoid prolonged heat or direct sunlight, and do not assume that a beer is suitable for aging just because it is “craft.” 2

Finally, here are a few common misconceptions:

  • “A 12° original wort concentration means 12% alcohol.” These are different measures; look at ABV for alcohol content.
  • “Twice the IBU means it will taste twice as bitter.” Perceived bitterness is also affected by sweetness, body, and overall balance.
  • “A banana aroma means bananas were added.” The characteristic banana note in German wheat beer can come from yeast fermentation.
  • “Dark beer is always high in alcohol.” Dark ingredients affect color, but only the alcohol label tells you the ABV.
  • “The cloudier, the better.” Some styles are meant to be hazy and others clear; judge them in the context of the style.
  • “If it tastes fruity like a soft drink, I can drink as much as I like.” Easy drinking does not change the alcohol content; pay attention to ABV and how much you consume.

This article discusses flavor and the experience of drinking, not drinking as health advice. Do not drive after drinking; you can understand these ideas without drinking at all.

Conclusion#

The next time you are making plans with friends and cannot decide where to go, consider a craft beer bar. Order something you like, have a chat, and play a few games.
