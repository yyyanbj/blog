0x00 Preface#

FedAvg looks simple from the server. Send a model to two clients, let them take two steps each, then average the results. If we sent them the global optimum, would the average still be there? I want to pick up from Part 31 and Part 42 and work that out. Keep the question in mind. We’ll test it later with two one-dimensional functions.

Start with everyone participating, with equal weights and equal numbers of local steps. That lets you see what shows up in the descent bound once the clients take a few steps. Then we’ll let the server draw only some clients and see what happens when the weights change. As before, for non-convex objectives we look at the average squared gradient. With strong convexity, PL lets us track the function-value gap.

The update is FedAvg from McMahan and colleagues3. I’ll round up a few constants in the proof, so when we get to a long expression we can still trace each term back to the step that produced it.

0x01 Objective and FedAvg Updates#

Global Objective#

First write down the objective we want the clients to optimize together. There are $N$ clients, with a local objective for client $i$ on its own data.

$$ f_i(w)=\mathbb E_{\xi\sim\mathcal D_i}[\ell(w;\xi)],\qquad f(w)=\sum_{i=1}^N p_i f_i(w),\qquad p_i>0,\quad\sum_{i=1}^Np_i=1. $$

If both clients have the same amount of data, these averages are easy to mix up. With different sample counts, first decide what gets equal weight. Equal weight per sample gives $p_i=n_i/\sum_j n_j$. Equal weight per client gives $p_i=1/N$. That choice sets the function we’re minimizing. Here we’re looking at optimization error. For differential privacy, we’d need to look separately at the mechanism and its guarantee.

I’ll derive the main bound with $p_i=1/N$. Once we have that, we can come back to client sampling and the choice of weights.

Local Steps and Communication Rounds#

Let’s write it in the order it happens. Use $t$ to count server communication rounds and $k$ to count local steps after the model goes out. In each round everyone receives the same model and runs local SGD for a number of steps satisfying $E\ge1$.

$$ \begin{aligned} w_{i,t}^{(0)}&=w_t,\\ w_{i,t}^{(k+1)}&=w_{i,t}^{(k)}-\eta g_{i,t}^{(k)},\qquad k=0,\ldots,E-1,\\ w_{t+1}&=\frac1N\sum_{i=1}^Nw_{i,t}^{(E)}. \end{aligned} $$

To see the server’s step, try adding up the local updates on each client first. Every step uses the same $\eta$, and the server directly averages the models. After summing, the result is one step in the aggregate direction below, with step size $\alpha=\eta E$.

$$ v_t=\frac1{NE}\sum_{i=1}^N\sum_{k=0}^{E-1}g_{i,t}^{(k)}, \qquad w_{t+1}=w_t-\alpha v_t. $$

In an implementation I’d count the actual optimizer steps, $E$. Two clients can both go through their local data once and take different numbers of steps if they have different data sizes. In this model, $T$ rounds are $ET$ local updates per client, for $NET$ batch gradients in total.

0x02 Assumptions and Error Decomposition#

Smoothness and Lower Bound#

Each $f_i$ is $L$-smooth in Euclidean space, so the average $f$ is also $L$-smooth. To sum the decreases later, assume $f(w)\ge f_{\inf}>-\infty$, write $\Delta_0=f(w_0)-f_{\inf}$, and treat $w_0$ as a fixed starting point. The non-convex proof uses the lower bound $f_{\inf}$. We’ll deal with attainment of a minimum in the strongly convex case.

Conditional Gradient Noise#

As in the last two posts, start by asking what we know when taking the expectation. Let $\mathcal F_t$ be the history before round $t$. The history $\mathcal H_{t,k}$ also includes the parameters and the events leading up to step $k$, before drawing its new batch. We can then write down the conditional noise.

$$ g_{i,t}^{(k)}=\nabla f_i(w_{i,t}^{(k)})+\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(k)}. $$ $$ \mathbb E[\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(k)}\mid\mathcal H_{t,k}]=0,\qquad \mathbb E[\|\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(k)}\|^2\mid\mathcal H_{t,k}] \le\frac{\sigma^2}{b}. $$

Use $b$ for the batch size at a local step and $\sigma^2$ for the common upper bound on single-sample noise variance. At the same step, the noises across clients are pairwise uncorrelated given the history. Across steps for one client, the conditional mean-zero property gives a martingale difference sequence. That’s what we’ll use on the cross terms from different steps.

The batch factor $1/b$ uses independent sampling or the corresponding covariance conditions. When walking through a random permutation, choosing samples by loss, or dealing with dropouts that affect sampling, I’d go back and check those conditions given the history.

Bounded Gradient Heterogeneity#

Now compare clients. Evaluate their gradients at the same parameter, subtract the global gradient, and average the squared differences. Assume a uniform bound on that quantity.

$$ \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^N\|\nabla f_i(w)-\nabla f(w)\|^2\le\zeta^2, \qquad \forall w. $$

Picture leaving one client in place and drawing a few batches. The variation you see is related to $\sigma^2$. Now put all the clients at the same parameter and compare their true gradients with the global gradient. The mean squared discrepancy is bounded by $\zeta^2$. A larger batch reduces the first source of variation. For the second, use $\sum_i(\nabla f_i-\nabla f)=0$ to split up the average squared gradient norm.

$$ \frac1N\sum_i\|\nabla f_i(w)\|^2 =\|\nabla f(w)\|^2+ \frac1N\sum_i\|\nabla f_i(w)-\nabla f(w)\|^2 \le\|\nabla f(w)\|^2+\zeta^2. $$

This assumption uses one bound at every parameter, including points far from the optimum. If we have a bound only in a region, first show that the iterates stay in it. Below I’ll use this discrepancy bound and smoothness to control the gradients.

Noise and Local Drift#

Back to the opening question. After we average, where does the step point? Take $h_t=\nabla f(w_t)$ as our reference from before the clients moved apart. Subtract it from the actual direction and look at what’s left.

$$ \begin{aligned} v_t&=h_t+r_t+e_t,\\ r_t&=\frac1{NE}\sum_{i,k} \left[\nabla f_i(w_{i,t}^{(k)})-\nabla f_i(w_t)\right],\\ e_t&=\frac1{NE}\sum_{i,k}\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(k)}. \end{aligned} $$

Follow the round we just described. The term $r_t$ measures how the gradients change as the clients move away. The term $e_t$ is the fluctuation from drawing batches at those points. Conditional unbiasedness and zero cross-covariances let us work out the second term.

$$ \mathbb E[e_t\mid\mathcal F_t]=0,\qquad \mathbb E[\|e_t\|^2\mid\mathcal F_t]\le \frac{\sigma^2}{bNE}. $$

For noise at different local steps, work backward through time with conditional expectations and the cross terms drop out. Pause at the drift, though. The parameters in $r_t$ already depend on earlier noise, so $\mathbb E\langle r_t,e_t\rangle=0$ takes an extra assumption. I’ll bound the two quantities together here.

0x03 Bounding Local Drift#

If the gradient difference is tricky to bound directly, start with how far the parameters have moved. Write $q=E-1$, work out the mean squared displacement at each step given the round-start history, then take the largest value for the round.

$$ D_{t,k}=\frac1N\sum_i \mathbb E[\|w_{i,t}^{(k)}-w_t\|^2\mid\mathcal F_t], \qquad M_t=\max_{0\le k\le q}D_{t,k}. $$

Here’s an index worth checking. We only count $k=0,\ldots,E-1$. After the final local update we send the parameter off to be averaged. The gradient evaluations for the round are already done. With $E=1$, we have $q=0$ and therefore $M_t=r_t=0$.

Add up the preceding updates. The displacement now contains a sum of true local gradients and a sum of noises. Use $|a+c|^2\le2|a|^2+2|c|^2$ to split the two sums and $|\sum_{s<k}a_s|^2\le k\sum_{s<k}|a_s|^2$ for the gradient sum. For the martingale differences, expanding the square leaves only the squared terms from each step.

$$ D_{t,k}\le \frac{2\eta^2k}{N}\sum_i\sum_{s<k} \mathbb E[\|\nabla f_i(w_{i,t}^{(s)})\|^2\mid\mathcal F_t] +\frac{2\eta^2k\sigma^2}{b}. $$

The first term still has gradients at the local parameters. Add and subtract the gradient at the start of the round. Smoothness bounds the difference, and the heterogeneity bound applies to the part at the starting point.

$$ \frac1N\sum_i \mathbb E[\|\nabla f_i(w_{i,t}^{(s)})\|^2\mid\mathcal F_t] \le2(\|h_t\|^2+\zeta^2)+2L^2D_{t,s}. $$ $$ D_{t,k}\le4\eta^2k^2(\|h_t\|^2+\zeta^2) +4\eta^2kL^2\sum_{s<k}D_{t,s} +\frac{2\eta^2k\sigma^2}{b}. $$

Now take the maximum over $0\le k\le q$. The same maximum bounds the displacements on the right, so the quantity shows up on both sides.

$$ M_t\le4\eta^2q^2(\|h_t\|^2+\zeta^2) +4\eta^2L^2q^2M_t+\frac{2\eta^2q\sigma^2}{b}. $$

With the same quantity on both sides, I first look at how small the right-hand coefficient is. The choice $0<\eta LE\le1/8$ gives $4\eta^2L^2q^2\le1/16$. That lets us move the term to the left, then round up the remaining constants.

$$ M_t\le8\eta^2q^2(\|h_t\|^2+\zeta^2) +\frac{4\eta^2q\sigma^2}{b}. $$

Now that we have the displacement, return to the definition of drift. Use Jensen to bound the square of the averaged gradient differences by the average of their squares, then use smoothness to turn each gradient difference into a displacement bound.

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[\|r_t\|^2\mid\mathcal F_t] &\le\frac{L^2}{E}\sum_{k=0}^{E-1}D_{t,k}\\ &\le8L^2\eta^2q^2(\|h_t\|^2+\zeta^2) +\frac{4L^2\eta^2q\sigma^2}{b}. \end{aligned} $$

Think again about whether the $NEb$ samples can be treated as one big batch. Looking at noise variance, we do get the factor $1/(NE)$. The gradients start from a common parameter, and then the clients move apart, so we also have to carry $r_t$.

0x04 A Non-convex Convergence Bound#

One-round Descent#

Now go back to the server. Put $w_{t+1}=w_t-\alpha v_t$ into the descent lemma for $f$ and look at a single communication round.

$$ f(w_{t+1})\le f(w_t)-\alpha\langle h_t,v_t\rangle +\frac{L\alpha^2}{2}\|v_t\|^2. $$

First condition on the history $\mathcal F_t$ at the start of the round, so $h_t$ is fixed. By $\mathbb E[e_t\mid\mathcal F_t]=0$, the inner product of $h_t$ with the noise disappears. Use the inequality below for the remaining inner product with the drift.

$$ |\langle h_t,r_t\rangle|\le\frac14\|h_t\|^2+\|r_t\|^2, \qquad \|h_t+r_t+e_t\|^2\le4\|h_t\|^2+4\|r_t\|^2+2\|e_t\|^2. $$

For the squared norm I bound $r_t$ and $e_t$ together. The norm inequality still holds when the two are dependent. Write $R_t=\mathbb E[|r_t|^2\mid\mathcal F_t]$ and collect the terms.

$$ \mathbb E[f(w_{t+1})\mid\mathcal F_t] \le f(w_t) -\alpha\left(\frac34-2L\alpha\right)\|h_t\|^2 +\alpha(1+2L\alpha)R_t +\frac{L\alpha^2\sigma^2}{bNE}. $$

Now we can ask how much descent is left in the round. By $L\alpha=L\eta E\le1/8$, the decrease coefficient is at least $1/2$, and the factor multiplying the drift satisfies $1+2L\alpha\le5/4$. Put the drift bound back in and see how much it takes away from the squared-gradient term.

$$ 10L^2\eta^2q^2\le\frac{10}{64}<\frac14. $$

That leaves at least $\alpha/4$ multiplying the decrease. Collect the remaining noise and heterogeneity terms into one quantity.

$$ A=\frac{L\eta\sigma^2}{bN} +\frac{5L^2\eta^2(E-1)\sigma^2}{b} +10L^2\eta^2(E-1)^2\zeta^2. $$

Keep this one line handy. When we want to find a point with a small gradient, we’ll sum it across the rounds. To track the gap to the minimum, we’ll use the relation from strong convexity on the same line.

$$ \mathbb E[f(w_{t+1})\mid\mathcal F_t] \le f(w_t)-\frac\alpha4\|\nabla f(w_t)\|^2+\alpha A. $$

Stationarity Guarantee#

defination

Under the assumptions above, with full participation and equal weights, take a step size satisfying $0<\eta\le1/(8LE)$ and a round count $T\ge1$. Then the average squared gradient norm has the following bound.
$$ \begin{aligned} \frac1T\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\mathbb E\|\nabla f(w_t)\|^2 &\le\frac{4\Delta_0}{\eta ET}+4A\\ &=\frac{4\Delta_0}{\eta ET} +\frac{4L\eta\sigma^2}{bN} +\frac{20L^2\eta^2(E-1)\sigma^2}{b} +40L^2\eta^2(E-1)^2\zeta^2. \end{aligned} $$

Use the same trick as in Part 3. Take total expectations, sum over $t=0,\ldots,T-1$, and the intervening function values cancel. Finish with $\mathbb E[f(w_T)]\ge f_{\inf}$. If you want one output from the points we’ve visited, draw $w_R$ independently and uniformly from these $T$ round-start parameters. The left-hand side is exactly $\mathbb E|\nabla f(w_R)|^2$ for that choice.

So I read this as an average over the round-start points. A bound for just the final $w_T$, or a claim about the global objective gap based on a small gradient, takes a further argument. Test accuracy calls for its own evaluation.

Reading the Four Terms#

TermMeaningImplication visible from this bound
$4\Delta_0/(\eta ET)$Optimization term for finite roundsWith other conditions fixed, more rounds reduce this term
$4L\eta\sigma^2/(bN)$Gradient noise after aggregationMore independent clients or larger batches can reduce this term
$20L^2\eta^2(E-1)\sigma^2/b$Drift accumulated by noise along local pathsCannot be eliminated solely by the $1/N$ reduction in aggregation
$40L^2\eta^2(E-1)^2\zeta^2$Drift caused by heterogeneous objectivesPerforming more local steps may increase this term

If a training run has flattened out, you might ask what more rounds will buy. At a fixed step size, increasing $T$ keeps shrinking the first term. The other three are still in the bound, and the actual error can be smaller. Try substituting $\zeta=0$ and noise can still send the clients apart. Try $\sigma=0$ and the different local objectives can create drift by themselves.

Fix $N,E,b$ and choose $\eta=c/\sqrt T$ with $0<c\le1/(8LE)$ for a run of length $T$. Substitution gives $O(T^{-1/2})+O(T^{-1})$. The step size stays fixed during that run. An online schedule $\eta_t$ needs its own analysis. If $E$ or $N$ grows with $T$, substitute those choices into every term too.

Here’s a check you can do quickly. With $E=1$, everyone evaluates gradients at the same point, so the drift vanishes and we’re back to synchronous mini-batch SGD. The unbiased proof in Part 31 gives tighter constants there. For the more general bound here, if you want a squared-gradient tolerance $\epsilon$, increasing $T$ only gives that guarantee when $\epsilon>4A$.

0x05 Strongly Convex Objectives#

Now suppose in addition that $f$ is $\mu$-strongly convex with a minimizer $w^\ast$. Write $f^\ast=f(w^\ast)$ and the function-value gap $\mathcal E_t=\mathbb E[f(w_t)]-f^\ast$. PL again lets us turn the squared gradient norm into that gap.

$$ \|\nabla f(w)\|^2\ge2\mu(f(w)-f^\ast), $$

Put that back into the one-round inequality and the familiar recursion comes back.

$$ \mathcal E_{t+1}\le \left(1-\frac{\alpha\mu}{2}\right)\mathcal E_t+\alpha A. $$

With $\mu\le L$ and the step-size restriction, we have $\rho=1-\alpha\mu/2\in(0,1)$. As in Part 3, write out the contribution added at each step. The right-hand side is a geometric sum.

defination

$$ \mathcal E_T\le\rho^T\mathcal E_0 +\frac{2A}{\mu}(1-\rho^T). $$

If I were showing someone this expression, I’d point first to the starting-point term, which shrinks geometrically with the rounds. At a fixed step size the remaining terms give the limiting bound $\limsup_T\mathcal E_T\le2A/\mu$. Convergence all the way to the minimum from this recursion depends on making the residual vanish.

If $0<2A/\mu<\epsilon<\mathcal E_0$, subtract the plateau from both sides, then use logarithms to count the rounds.

$$ T\ge\left\lceil \frac{\log\left((\mathcal E_0-2A/\mu)/(\epsilon-2A/\mu)\right)}{-\log\rho} \right\rceil. $$

When $A=0$, read the bound directly as $\rho^T\mathcal E_0$. For a tolerance below the residual plateau, we need another argument beyond adding rounds. For a strongly convex analysis with decreasing step sizes, see Li and colleagues at ICLR 20204. I’d line up their time indices, assumptions, and aggregation scheme before comparing their $O(1/T)$ result.

0x06 A Deterministic Drift Example#

Now we can go back to the question from the start. Remove the noise, give the two clients a one-dimensional function each, and deliberately send them the global optimum. Work through one round to see what comes back.

Give the two clients equal weight, take $0<a<1/\sqrt2$, and use these two local objectives.

$$ f_1(w)=\frac12w^2+a(\sin w+\cos w),\qquad f_2(w)=\frac12w^2-a(\sin w+\cos w). $$ $$ f(w)=\frac12w^2,\qquad w^\ast=0,\qquad \nabla f_{1,2}(w)=w\pm a(\cos w-\sin w). $$

Look at the curvature of each function. Both are $L$-smooth and strongly convex, with $L=1+\sqrt2a$ and strong convexity parameter $1-\sqrt2a$. Subtract the gradients to get a heterogeneity bound over the whole space.

$$ \frac12\sum_{i=1}^2|\nabla f_i(w)-\nabla f(w)|^2 =a^2(\cos w-\sin w)^2\le2a^2. $$

Start at $w_t=0$ and give each client two deterministic GD steps. Try writing down the first step. One goes to $-\eta a$, the other to $\eta a$. Evaluate once more at those two new positions, then average the resulting parameters.

$$ w_{t+1}=-\eta a\sin(\eta a). $$

Now we can see what happened. With a sufficiently small positive step size, the average has moved away. With $E=1$, both sides evaluate at $w=0$ and the gradients cancel. On the extra step each client evaluates where it has already moved, and that leaves a difference.

If $0<\eta\le1/L$, each local GD map is a contraction, and averaging the two-step maps still gives a contraction. Repeating the round leads to a unique attracting fixed point. We just saw that zero moves under the map, so that fixed point lies away from the global optimum. The heterogeneity assumption holds in this example. With $\eta\le1/(16L)$, the step size also meets the main theorem’s condition for $E=2$.

from math import cos, sin, sqrt

a, eta = 0.25, 0.04
assert eta * 2 * (1 + sqrt(2) * a) <= 1 / 8


def fedavg_round(w, steps=2):
    local = []
    for sign in (1, -1):
        x = w
        for _ in range(steps):
            x -= eta * (x + sign * a * (cos(x) - sin(x)))
        local.append(x)
    return sum(local) / 2


assert abs(fedavg_round(0) + eta * a * sin(eta * a)) < 1e-12
assert abs(fedavg_round(0, steps=1)) < 1e-12
w = 0.0
for _ in range(2000):
    w = fedavg_round(w)
assert abs(w) > 1e-6
assert abs(fedavg_round(w) - w) < 1e-12
print(f"FedAvg fixed point: {w:.8f}; global optimum: 0")

Try running it if you want. The script checks the one-step case first, then finds where the two-step round settles. Compare the printed fixed point with zero to see the result for these two functions.

0x07 Partial Participation and Aggregation Weights#

Client Sampling Adds Another Variance Term#

Change one more thing and let the server pick only some clients each round. Start with $E=1$ and keep the equal-weight objective. Draw $m$ of $N>1$ clients uniformly without replacement, then draw the batches independently of that selection. The direction we get is $\hat g_t=m^{-1}\sum_{i\in S_t}g_i(w_t)$.

$$ \mathbb E[\hat g_t\mid\mathcal F_t]=\nabla f(w_t), $$ $$ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E[\|\hat g_t-\nabla f(w_t)\|^2\mid\mathcal F_t] &\le\frac{\sigma^2}{bm} +\frac{N-m}{m(N-1)}\cdot\frac1N\sum_i \|\nabla f_i(w_t)-\nabla f(w_t)\|^2\\ &\le\frac{\sigma^2}{bm}+\frac{N-m}{m(N-1)}\zeta^2. \end{aligned} $$

It’s easy to mix up the two draws here. Set aside which batch each client gets and count just which clients are selected. Fix $\mathcal F_t$, write $a_i=\nabla f_i(w_t)-\nabla f(w_t)$ for each local discrepancy, and let $I_i=\mathbf 1_{{i\in S_t}}$ mark inclusion. Using $\sum_i a_i=0$, all we need next are the probabilities of including one client and including a pair.

$$ \mathbb E[I_i\mid\mathcal F_t]=\frac mN,\qquad \mathbb E[I_iI_j\mid\mathcal F_t]=\frac{m(m-1)}{N(N-1)},\quad i\ne j. $$ $$ \sum_{i\ne j}\langle a_i,a_j\rangle=-\sum_i\|a_i\|^2. $$

Now expand the square. Put the two probabilities above into the squared terms for individual clients and the cross terms for distinct clients, respectively.

$$ \begin{aligned} \mathbb E\left[\left\|\frac1m\sum_i I_i a_i\right\|^2\middle|\mathcal F_t\right] &=\frac1{m^2}\left(\frac mN-\frac{m(m-1)}{N(N-1)}\right)\sum_i\|a_i\|^2\\ &=\frac{N-m}{m(N-1)}\cdot\frac1N\sum_i\|a_i\|^2. \end{aligned} $$

Once we condition on $S_t$, the sample gradient noise has mean zero, so the cross term with the client selection error drops out. Try two cases as a check. At $m=N$, the client sampling term is zero. With replacement, the selection calculation changes and gives $1/m$ for the heterogeneity coefficient.

We’ve accounted for both sources of variance now. Put their bound into the one-step inequality from Part 3. With $0<\eta\le1/L$, we get the bound below.

$$ \frac1T\sum_{t<T}\mathbb E\|\nabla f(w_t)\|^2 \le\frac{2\Delta_0}{\eta T} +L\eta\left(\frac{\sigma^2}{bm} +\frac{N-m}{m(N-1)}\zeta^2\right). $$

With $N=1$, the only choice is $m=1$. We pick the same client every time and the sampling error is zero, so skip the coefficient involving $N-1$. For $E>1$, changing the count from $N$ to $m$ accounts for who is selected. We still need to go back to where the selected clients evaluate their gradients on the extra steps.

Extending the Bound to Multiple Local Steps#

Keep the uniform draw without replacement independent of the round’s new batch noise. The $m$ selected clients start at $w_t$, take the same $E$ steps, and the server averages their models. Now split the update direction as we did before.

$$ \chi_m=\frac{N-m}{m(N-1)},\qquad u_t=\frac1m\sum_{i\in S_t}\nabla f_i(w_t)-h_t, $$ $$ \begin{aligned} r_t^{(m)}&=\frac1{mE}\sum_{i\in S_t}\sum_{k=0}^{E-1} \bigl[\nabla f_i(w_{i,t}^{(k)})-\nabla f_i(w_t)\bigr],\\ e_t^{(m)}&=\frac1{mE}\sum_{i\in S_t}\sum_{k=0}^{E-1}\varepsilon_{i,t}^{(k)},\\ v_t^{(m)}&=h_t+u_t+r_t^{(m)}+e_t^{(m)}. \end{aligned} $$

You can work out $u_t$ before the clients have taken even one step. They all have the same parameter, and the only variation comes from who was picked. Combine it with their sample noise into $z_t=u_t+e_t^{(m)}$. We just found the variance for the first part and can handle the second using the same stepwise noise argument as before.

$$ \mathbb E[z_t\mid\mathcal F_t]=0,\qquad \mathbb E[\|z_t\|^2\mid\mathcal F_t] \le\chi_m\zeta^2+\frac{\sigma^2}{bmE}. $$

After conditioning on the selected set, $\mathbb E[e_t^{(m)}\mid\mathcal F_t,S_t]=0$, so the cross term between $u_t$ and $e_t^{(m)}$ is zero. For the local drift $r_t^{(m)}$, we’ll still bound the squared norm together with $z_t$.

What remains is to see how much of the drift proof still goes through with a subset of clients. Average the displacement over the selected clients, then take expectations over selection and noise.

$$ D_{t,k}^{(m)}=\mathbb E\left[\frac1m\sum_{i\in S_t} \|w_{i,t}^{(k)}-w_t\|^2\middle|\mathcal F_t\right]. $$

At the start of the round, the local gradients are already set by the current parameter. Each client has probability $m/N$ of appearing in $S_t$. Taking the expectation therefore gives us the average over all clients again.

$$ \mathbb E\left[\frac1m\sum_{i\in S_t}\|\nabla f_i(w_t)\|^2 \middle|\mathcal F_t\right] =\frac1N\sum_i\|\nabla f_i(w_t)\|^2 \le\|h_t\|^2+\zeta^2. $$

Now expand the selected clients’ paths as in 0x03, then average over the round’s selection and noise. This gives the same recursion for $D_{t,k}^{(m)}$. Use Jensen at the end to turn the displacements into a drift bound.

$$ \mathbb E[\|r_t^{(m)}\|^2\mid\mathcal F_t] \le8L^2\eta^2(E-1)^2(\|h_t\|^2+\zeta^2) +\frac{4L^2\eta^2(E-1)\sigma^2}{b}. $$

Now return to the one-round decrease in 0x04. Replace $e_t$ with $z_t$ and keep bounding drift and noise together in the squared term. Use $\chi_m\zeta^2+\sigma^2/(bmE)$ for the variance and collect the residual.

$$ A_m=\frac{L\eta\sigma^2}{bm} +L\eta E\chi_m\zeta^2 +\frac{5L^2\eta^2(E-1)\sigma^2}{b} +10L^2\eta^2(E-1)^2\zeta^2. $$

defination

With this uniform selection without replacement and step size satisfying $0<\eta\le1/(8LE)$, we get the non-convex bound below.
$$ \frac1T\sum_{t<T}\mathbb E\|\nabla f(w_t)\|^2 \le\frac{4\Delta_0}{\eta ET}+4A_m. $$
Add strong convexity and apply PL as in 0x05. The contraction factor is still $\rho=1-\eta E\mu/2$.
$$ \mathcal E_T\le\rho^T\mathcal E_0+\frac{2A_m}{\mu}(1-\rho^T). $$

To check the long expression, plug in two simple cases. For $m=N$ we have $\chi_m=0$ and $A_m=A$, back to full participation. With $E=1$ only noise and selection error remain, and the previous calculation gives a wider step-size range and tighter constants. In the corner case $N=m=1$, set $\chi_m=0$ separately.

Compare $L\eta E\chi_m\zeta^2$ and $\eta^2(E-1)^2\zeta^2$. The first can arise even at a common parameter, since we’re drawing different clients. The second comes from taking several local steps. Increasing $m$ reduces the first term. To see whether the second gets a factor of $1/m$, go back to the local paths. For state-dependent selection, repeated cohorts, different weights, or different local step counts, check the sampling and update assumptions of this proof again.

Weighting Can Change the Target#

Back to the weights. Suppose you want to optimize $f=\sum_i p_i f_i$ and the server draws one client uniformly each round, using that client’s entire update. With $E=1$, work out the expectation and you get $N^{-1}\sum_i\nabla f_i$. That gives each client the same weight. To target $\nabla f$, the weights and the draw have to agree.

Try the two functions $f_1(w)=w^2/2$ and $f_2(w)=(w-2)^2/2$ with $p_1=0.9,p_2=0.1$. Differentiate the weighted objective and the equally weighted one. Their minimizers are $0.2$ and $1$, respectively. Before comparing rates, check which of those optima each update is heading for.

If we know client $i$’s inclusion probability $\pi_i>0$ and can evaluate the gradients at a common $w$, the following Horvitz–Thompson correction gives an unbiased estimate.

$$ \hat g(w)=\sum_{i\in S}\frac{p_i}{\pi_i}g_i(w), \qquad\mathbb E[\hat g(w)]=\sum_i p_i\nabla f_i(w). $$

When I read the code, I look first at what we divide by. The argument used assumptions on selection and fresh sample noise. If the denominator is the sum of selected weights, it changes with the draw and the resulting ratio is generally biased. With importance weighting, check the cost in variance too.

Selecting by availability, speed, or loss can change which objective the gradients correspond to and what happens to the noise assumptions. As with selecting the fastest workers in Part 42, look at the gradients after selection.

0x08 Communication, Drift, and Corrections#

Local Work Is Not Free Progress#

If you ask what more local steps save, let’s fix the total local update count $H=ET$. The first term is then $4\Delta_0/(\eta H)$, and increasing $E$ cuts the number of communication rounds. At the same step size it also raises the drift terms, and $\eta LE\le1/8$ lowers the largest step size we can choose. I’d include all those changes when comparing two values of $E$.

Another way to look at it is to fix the round’s effective step size $\alpha=\eta E$. Substituting $\eta=\alpha/E$ makes the factor $\eta^2(E-1)^2$ in the heterogeneity term approach $\alpha^2$, so it remains as we add local steps. For elapsed time we also need to account for downloading, uploading, local training, and waiting. The post on distributed machine learning efficiency5 gives a way to do that. Use a timing model for the actual devices in place of its homogeneous-worker assumption.

Choosing Parameters from the Bound#

To pick a step size from the bound, work backward as in Part 2. Fix $T,E,m,b$ for this run, leave the learning rate blank for a moment, and see what sort of function we have on the right.

$$ B(\eta)=\frac{c_0}{\eta}+c_1\eta+c_2\eta^2, \qquad 0<\eta\le\frac1{8LE}, $$ $$ \begin{aligned} c_0&=\frac{4\Delta_0}{ET},\\ c_1&=\frac{4L\sigma^2}{bm}+4LE\chi_m\zeta^2,\\ c_2&=\frac{20L^2(E-1)\sigma^2}{b} +40L^2(E-1)^2\zeta^2. \end{aligned} $$

If $c_0>0$ and $c_1+c_2>0$, differentiate with respect to the step size and clear the denominator. The unconstrained minimum occurs at the unique positive root below.

$$ -c_0+c_1\eta^2+2c_2\eta^3=0. $$

The left side increases over the positive half-line, so find the root by bisection and cap it at $1/(8LE)$. With $c_2=0,c_1>0$ this reduces to the familiar $\eta=\sqrt{c_0/c_1}$. With $c_1=c_2=0$, take the largest step size in the allowed interval. If $c_0=0$, go back to the initial residual. The positive-root argument used a positive starting gap.

That’s why I’d look at the quadratic term before interpreting the step-size scaling through $\eta\propto1/\sqrt T$. In practice we usually don’t know $L,\Delta_0,\sigma^2,\zeta^2$, and the constants here were rounded up. I use this expression to understand the trends in the bound.

If we were running a comparison together, I’d start with one local step per round, then increase $E$ and see what the curves do. Match the total local steps or elapsed time first and record the communication rounds. If we took more local steps and also ran more rounds, we’d need another run on a matched budget to tell what made the difference.

To measure $\zeta^2$, compare the gradients at a common parameter. Differences in label proportions, sample sizes, and minimizers are separate things. To relate one of those to gradient discrepancy, go back to the definition. With a Dirichlet split, the concentration parameter controls the split. Relating it to $\zeta^2$ takes additional assumptions on the functions and data.

To look at the two errors in an experiment, use the same server parameter $w_t$ to estimate each local gradient. A single finite batch still includes sampling noise. I’d keep the parameter fixed and sample again, use a larger diagnostic batch, or estimate the variance explicitly before comparing clients. In cross-device training, also count the extra communication and data access.

FedProx and SCAFFOLD#

Let’s look at two ways to change the local update itself. FedProx6 adds a proximal term inside each round.

$$ f_i(w)+\frac\lambda2\|w-w_t\|^2,\qquad\lambda\ge0. $$

The quadratic is easy to read. The farther a local parameter moves from the server’s starting point, the more it pays in its objective. To quantify how much the drift is reduced, we’d check the objectives and the accuracy of the local solves, then work from the modified update.

SCAFFOLD7 changes the direction by applying a control-variate correction to each client’s gradient.

$$ w_i\leftarrow w_i-\eta\bigl(g_i(w_i)-c_i+c\bigr). $$

Try an idealized case first. Set $c_i=\nabla f_i(w_t)$ and $c=\nabla f(w_t)$ at the start of the round and evaluate at $w_i=w_t$. Subtracting the local gradient and adding the global gradient gives exactly the right mean. As the local parameter moves, the change of location introduces another discrepancy. The actual algorithm estimates and updates the control variates, so a further bound needs to include estimation error and sampling.

0x09 What the Analysis Guarantees#

When I come back to this later, I want to remember where the terms came from. The full-participation bound uses synchronization, equal weights, equal step counts, conditional unbiasedness, and uniform heterogeneity. In the non-convex case we average the squared gradients along the trajectory. Under strong convexity the gap bound heads toward a plateau. With some clients we add the term for sampling without replacement. If the weights change, go back and calculate the expected update again.

To read these expressions against a training run, I’d start by checking smoothness, batch sampling, and participation. With Adam, momentum, unequal step counts, an asynchronous server, or a non-smooth activation, revisit whichever assumption or update changed. Validation accuracy, generalization, fairness, and privacy each call for their own evaluation.

Next time you show me a training curve, I’d first check the objective weights and $N,m,E,b,\eta$ with you. Then I’d ask who was picked each round, how far the clients moved, and how long the server waited. With those details beside the curve, we can talk about what we got from more local steps, different sampling, or another communication round.
